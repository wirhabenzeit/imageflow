## What to remember

The white cottage looked small in the photograph. From the road it felt like the only thing for miles, though we had passed two other houses a few minutes earlier. A pile of wood stood against its wall. We took one picture and kept walking.

The shore was mostly stone. A line of seaweed marked the earlier tide, and tiny pools reflected the sky. We stood back from the water and watched the wind turn the surface from smooth grey to a scattering of bright points.

1. Follow the old road past the bend.
2. Stop for a view of the ridge before the path turns inland.
3. Leave enough time to walk back while there is light.

The useful notes were mostly about where to stand and when to come back. The rest was already in the pictures.

## On the light

Light this far north does not behave the way it does at home. In the evening it comes in low and sideways and stays there for hours, so that every stone on the beach throws a long shadow and the grass on the slopes turns the colour of brass. Photographs taken at ten at night look like late afternoon. Photographs taken at two in the morning look like dawn, because they are.

We learned to stop checking the time. Dinner happened when we were hungry, and the walk back from the shore happened when the light went flat, which on clear days it barely did at all. On cloudy days everything softened and the colours went quiet, and those turned out to be some of the best pictures: grey water, grey sky, and a single red boathouse in between.

## Small things

- The smell of the birches after rain.
- A sheep standing on the roof of an old turf shed.
- The ferry horn, twice a day, carrying across the whole fjord.
- Cloudberries not yet ripe, hard and pale in the moss.
- How quiet it was at the top of the ridge once the wind dropped.

None of these made a good photograph on its own. Together they are the reason the photographs mean anything at all.

## For next time

Bring a second pair of boots, because one pair never dries. Bring a lighter lens for long walks, and leave the heavy one in the car more often. Write the notes on the same day, before the details blur into each other. And plan one day with no plan at all, because the best afternoon of the trip was the one we had kept free for bad weather that never came.
